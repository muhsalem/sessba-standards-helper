What is SSESBA

Four roles in one reference system

SSESBA is not a single fatwa — it is an integrated reference system: a standards framework, a classification system, a compliance tool, and an evidence-backed knowledge base, all working together.

Standards framework

Unified Shariah controls for every sector and activity, in a consistent 22-section model.

Classification system

Dual coding that pins each activity to its sector and its compliance band.

Compliance tool

An applicable, auditable scorecard for institutional use.

Knowledge reference

A documented package with evidence gradings and resolution numbers.

Why it exists

The gaps it was built to close

Shariah judgments on economic activity are scattered, inconsistent, and hard to apply at scale. SSESBA replaces ad-hoc rulings with one disciplined, comparable reference.

01

Institutions apply different yardsticks, so results are not comparable.

02

The same activity earns conflicting rulings across bodies.

03

Hard to assess activities

Modern and hybrid business models resist simple permitted/prohibited labels.

04

Need for a professional reference

Markets and regulators need an auditable, citable standard.

Shariah classification

Six levels on one unified compliance scale

The numeric score resolves into six precise levels. The eligibility gate applies first; the level is then grouped into an operational band and combined with S1–S4 risk to reach the final verdict.

95 — 100

Fully compliant

Documented compliance across the axes with no observed material deviation.

85 — 94

Substantially compliant

Strong compliance with limited observations that do not affect the core activity.

75 — 84

Compliant with conditions

Acceptable subject to defined conditions and a monitored remediation or purification plan.

60 — 74

Material correction is required before accreditation can be considered.

45 — 59

Non-compliant

0 — 44

⊕How compliance applies to non-profits

The eligibility gate checks the lawfulness of the purpose, the funding sources, and the disbursement channels. The six axes are adapted: revenues → purity of funding sources; financing → lawful investment of the endowment/surplus; operations → integrity of programmes and disbursement; governance → fiduciary and Shariah oversight of funds (e.g. waqf trusteeship); disclosure → transparency of sources and uses. Profit is not the measure — lawful stewardship of funds is.

Version policy & migration path

Built on ISIC Rev.4 — with a declared path to Rev.5

The UN Statistical Commission endorsed ISIC Rev.5 in 2023; countries are expected to adapt their national classifications between 2025–2026 and implement from 2027. This standard is therefore built on Rev.4 — still the version in live national use during the transition — while a migration to Rev.5 is declared policy, not an afterthought.

Phase 1 — now
Rev.4 spine + published Rev.4↔Rev.5 crosswalk for the affected categories.
Phase 2 — dual coding
Each activity carries both codes during the transition window.
Phase 3 — Rev.5 primary
Rev.5 becomes the spine as national implementation completes; Rev.4 retained for historical comparability.

Known Rev.5 impacts already flagged: Division 45 (trade & repair of motor vehicles) is removed and its activities redistributed; Section J is split; new classes cover cloud/computing infrastructure and web search portals.

Evaluation methodology

Points, risk, and a disciplined decision

The verdict is never a single opinion. It is assembled from a weighted score, a Shariah-risk tier, and a fixed decision rule.

1 · Points system

Six weighted axes (25/25/20/15/10/5) produce a 0–100 score, gated by activity legitimacy.

2 · Shariah-risk system

An S1–S4 tier captures ruling maturity, referral status, and madhhab divergence.

3 · Decision rule

Gate → score → band → risk overlay → final, documented verdict.

Sectoral weight profiles

Weights are calibrated per sector — finance lifts financing and governance — instead of one global vector.

Per-axis floors

Conjunctive minimums stop a strong axis from masking a critically weak one.

Anchored rubric

A published 0/25/50/75/100 rubric per axis ensures reproducible, inter-rater-consistent scoring.

Published scoring rubric

Anchored levels for reproducible scoring

Each of the six axes is scored against the same five anchored levels — so the verdict is auditable and consistent across different assessors, not left to individual discretion.

ScoreAnchored description
100Full, documented compliance — no observed deviation.
75Good compliance with limited, manageable observations.
50Partial compliance requiring clear remediation.
25Weak compliance with multiple deviations.
0A substantive, explicit breach.

The axis score is the rubric level; the weighted sum across the six axes (25/25/20/15/10/5) yields the 0–100 result, subject to per-axis floors and the eligibility gate.

Reliability & scientific method

Anchored in recognised references

The framework declares its method openly: weighing between madhhabs with maslaha, under the maqasid, with an explicit facilitation tendency — no implied consensus where none exists.

ISIC Rev.4

The structural spine for sector and activity classification — with a declared migration path to Rev.5.

AAOIFI

The governing reference for the financial sector (K): Shariah, accounting, auditing, governance and ethics standards for Islamic banks, takaful operators and Islamic financial institutions. Includes screening thresholds — e.g. interest-bearing debt ≤30% of assets, impermissible income ≤5% of revenues (adoption and thresholds vary by jurisdiction).

OIC/SMIIC

The OIC standards body (Istanbul): halal standards for food (OIC/SMIIC 1), tourism, pharmaceuticals & cosmetics, the halal supply chain, plus certification and accreditation requirements (OIC/SMIIC 2 & 3) — applied per sector and activity.

Fiqh academies

Resolutions cited by number, e.g. cultured meat (265) and the crypto referral (237).

Islamic economics

Contract theory, revenue purity, and financing structures.

Governance

Board oversight and Shariah audit alignment.

Evidence base

Hadith gradings with chain analysis; marfu' separated from mawquf.

Emerging digital models

Built for the age of agentic systems

The standard classifies the model by its axes, not its name — so it re-evaluates as fast as digital business models change.

Crowdfunding

Ruled by its contract form

Equity (musharakah/mudarabah) is judged on partnership purity; interest-bearing lending fails the gate; donation/reward models are judged on transparency and absence of gharar.

Sharing platforms

Liability for what they host

Revenue purity (share of non-permitted income) and operations (admission and oversight controls) set the band — not a blunt yes/no.

Virtual influencers

Honest, gharar-free promotion

Truthfulness, no promotion of the impermissible, no gharar in advertising deals, and privacy — risk rises where disclosure is absent.

Agentic AI

Automation assists, it does not issue fatwa

The machine-readable GSCS code enables automated first-pass screening, while ijtihad stays with the human Shariah board and oversight remains on high-risk tiers.

Honest positioning

What is genuinely new here

Shariah screening is not new — continuous compliance scoring exists in the equity literature. The contribution is a shift of scope, stated plainly without overclaiming.

What it does NOT claim

  • Not the first to score Shariah compliance — DJIMI (1999), Malaysia's SAC, and a 2026 continuous-index paper precede it.
  • Not a consensus ruling — it declares a facilitation tendency, not ijma'.
  • Not yet an adopted, binding standard — it is an advanced research framework.

What it DOES contribute

  • Moves from equity/portfolio screening to a whole-economy, activity-based reference standard.
  • Built on the ISIC activity backbone — not on market indices.
  • Six axes beyond debt/revenue ratios, an independent risk tier, and a machine-readable code.

Distinct from the 2026 continuous-index (CSCI) work: that measures equities on a [0,1] portfolio-screening index from the same financial ratios. This standard instead classifies activities and business models across the whole economy on the ISIC backbone, adds an independent Shariah-risk tier and an eligibility gate separate from scoring, and emits a machine-readable code — a different object, not a re-weighting of the same screen.

FAQ

Questions, answered plainly

What is the difference between SSESBA and GSCS?
SSESBA is the body of Shariah standards for each sector; GSCS is the coding layer that classifies any activity and places it on the compliance scale. One sets the rules, the other expresses the result.
On what basis are activities classified?
On three layers: (1) a structural spine — ISIC Rev.4 — which fixes each activity's single home; (2) an extension layer for distinctions ISIC cannot express (e.g. class 6419 lumps conventional and Islamic banks together, so the eligibility gate, the six axes and the entity dimension carry that distinction); (3) a crosswalk layer to GICS/NACE for interoperability and display. ISIC is the spine, not the whole standard.
How is the compliance score calculated?
From six weighted axes — contracts 25%, revenues 25%, financing 20%, operations 15%, governance 10%, disclosure 5% — after the activity passes the eligibility gate.
Does the financial sector follow AAOIFI standards?
Yes. The financial & insurance sector (K) is governed by AAOIFI — the accounting, auditing, governance, ethics and Shariah standards for Islamic banks, takaful operators and Islamic financial institutions — applied alongside the activity's six weighted axes. Legal adoption and exact screening thresholds vary by jurisdiction.
Do you adopt OIC/SMIIC standards, and how are they mapped?
Yes. Halal sectors are anchored to the OIC/SMIIC family per activity: food (OIC/SMIIC 1), tourism, pharmaceuticals and cosmetics, and the halal supply chain — with OIC/SMIIC 2 & 3 governing certification and accreditation bodies. These apply alongside the six weighted axes, while finance is anchored to AAOIFI.
Is the result a binding fatwa?
No. It is a standards framework, not a binding ruling. The current text is an advanced working draft under collective scholarly review.
What is your jurisprudential method?
Weighing between the madhhabs with consideration of maslaha, under the framework of the maqasid, with a declared tendency toward facilitation — stated as a methodological choice, not implied consensus.
How are emerging activities like digital assets handled?
With referral status and elevated risk (S3+), consistent with the fiqh academy resolution that recommends further study without a definitive ruling.
Is social responsibility part of the score?
No. It is treated as aspirational and excluded from the binding compliance score.
